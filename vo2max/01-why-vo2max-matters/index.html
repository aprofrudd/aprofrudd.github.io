The number that predicts how long you live

In 2002, researchers followed 6,213 men who had walked on a treadmill. Years later they checked who was still alive. The thing that predicted it best was not their cholesterol, their blood pressure, their weight, or whether they smoked. It was how far they could walk.

Why anyone cares about this

Doctors have a long list of things they measure to work out how likely you are to die. This study asked which one actually wins.

If you go to a doctor for a check-up, they will weigh you, take your blood pressure, ask whether you smoke, and send off a blood sample for cholesterol and blood sugar. Every one of those is a genuine risk factor. Every one of them is on the list because good research put it there.

What they will almost certainly not do is find out how fit you are.

In 2002 a team led by Jonathan Myers published a study that made this look like an odd omission. They had treadmill test results for 6,213 men, and they had records of who had died in the years afterwards. So they could ask a simple question: of everything we measure, which one tells us the most?

The answer was fitness. Not by a small margin, and not only in the healthy men. This module walks through how they got there, and lets you pull the results apart yourself.

The headline Among the healthy men in this study, only two of the things measured clearly predicted who would die: how fit they were, and how much they had smoked. Blood pressure, diabetes, cholesterol and heart rate did not separate the survivors from the rest. You will see exactly why in section 05.

What on earth is a MET?

Every number in this study is measured in METs. Here is the whole idea in one sentence, and then something to play with.

A MET is just a multiple of how hard your body is working when you are doing nothing at all. Sitting still in a chair is 1 MET. Something that takes twice as much oxygen is 2 METs. Ten times as much is 10 METs. That is the entire concept.
